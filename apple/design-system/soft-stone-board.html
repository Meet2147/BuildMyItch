The rule

Flip that switch. Nothing should break.

Elevation tells you what is a surface, what is a container, what is a control. It never tells you what is selected, urgent, done, or disabled — those are always carried by contrast, weight, or a glyph. So the app has to stay completely usable with every shadow at zero, and it does: turn relief off and hairlines take over. There is a debug flag in the real app that does exactly this, and a screenshot suite that runs in it. If a state vanishes when you flatten, it was encoded in relief, and that's a bug.

The press is the point

Raised → carved over 120ms, back over 220ms with a slight overshoot. That transition is the button feedback, and it's the only reason to use this language at all. A .impact(.soft) haptic on the down transition — never on release.

Light comes from the top-left

On every element, in both apps, in every locale. Offsets are always (−x,−y) highlight and (+x,+y) shadow, and they are not mirrored for RTL — light doesn't flip when the language does. One inconsistent element and the material stops reading as material.

Material

A warm ground, not a blue one

Almost every neumorphism demo uses a cool blue-grey. On an OLED panel it reads cold and plastic. Pulling a few points of yellow in makes the same effect read as paper or clay. In dark mode the highlight becomes a lifted grey rather than white — a white highlight on a dark ground reads as a glow, not a bevel — so the whole effect gets subtler after dark, which is correct.

Type

SF Pro, used properly. Monospaced digits everywhere a number changes. Tightened tracking on the big clock. Small caps for metadata — the trick that makes a minimal UI look typeset instead of unfinished.

Colour

One accent per app, in at most three places per screen. Semantic reds and greens are desaturated to sit inside the material — full-saturation system red on a soft grey ground looks like a rendering bug.

Motion

One spring, three tunings: snap for controls, settle for rows and sheets, drift for ambient. Nothing on an interactive path lasts over 500ms. Motion is causal — a sheet grows from the control you tapped.

Sill · the todo app

Capture is easy. Deciding is the job.

Every todo app is good at capture and punts the hard part back to you — here are your tags, your priorities, your 87 overdue items, good luck. The list becomes a monument to what you haven't done and you stop opening it. Sill takes the deciding job: you throw things in with no structure, and once a day it produces one screen — today, with a realistic number of things on it. Everything else is deliberately hard to look at.

One sentence in, real tasks out

Pick up parcel · errand Gym · 30m · errand Book flights · admin · 20m Reply to Sam · social

Nobody thinks in single tasks — people think in dumps. Handling one comma-separated brain-dump correctly is the whole difference between capture and typing into a form. Parsing runs on-device; task text never leaves the phone.

Energy, not priority

Priority flags fail because everything becomes P1. Sill classifies by the shape of attention a thing needs, which is observable and doesn't inflate.

DeepUninterrupted hour+ → your best focus window, max two a day
AdminShort, boring, low stakes → batched into one afternoon block
ErrandRequires being somewhere → grouped by location
SocialRequires another person → inside their working hours
IdleDoable in a queue → lives on the widget, never scheduled

A day that actually fits

Sill reads your calendar and knows how much unscheduled time you have. Ninety free minutes means ninety minutes of tasks — three items, not eleven. Drag between bands to re-plan and the rest reflows. That's the entire planning interface.

The backlog is not a debt

Nothing goes red. Nothing grows an overdue badge. An old task loses contrast on a curve, and at thirty days Sill asks once, on a quiet card: still real? Letting go isn't deleting — it goes somewhere you never have to look at.

Aubade · the alarm app

The most-used app on the phone, and the least considered.

Third parties couldn't fix the alarm clock for a decade, because an app that wasn't Clock couldn't ring through silent mode or a Focus. AlarmKit changed that, and the category still hasn't produced a beautiful one. Aubade does three things: wakes you at the right moment rather than the exact minute you typed, wakes you gradually with light and sound that start below your waking threshold, and owns the night before, because that's what actually determines the morning.

Left: the wake ritual — hold the ring and a slow haptic paces an inhale-hold-exhale for eight seconds. You can't do that in your sleep, but it's calm rather than punitive, and it leaves you more awake than shouting at a QR code does. Right: the night face. Amber-only ink at near-invisible brightness, relief dialled almost to nothing because heavy shadows at low brightness just read as smudges. It's a bedside clock worth leaving out.

You set “by 6:40”, not 6:40

Aubade rings at the best moment inside a window and never later than the hard time. With the phone on the mattress it uses movement clustering; with the phone across the room it rings at the hard time and says so during setup. Pretending to detect sleep from a phone across the room is the kind of lie that makes an app feel cheap.

Sound that starts under your threshold

Generative, not looped, so there's no seam and no moment you learn to recognise as “the bit before it gets loud”. It opens at 15% on a low sustained tone; layers enter one at a time; volume ramps shallow for the first 60% and steep at the end. Six palettes — Glass, Ember, Rainfall, Bell Garden, Low Strings, Tide — and a five-minute wake is 40KB of parameters, not five minutes of audio.

Keystone · sync

Sync less. Derive more.

SwiftData locally, CloudKit private database for transport, the user's own Apple Account for identity. No login screen, no server, no subscription to pay for one. Two apps, two containers, two very different jobs: Sill genuinely syncs across three devices, while Aubade has exactly one writer, so its “sync” is a backup that happens to use the same machinery.

Firing is never the network's job

Every device schedules its own OS-level alarm from local data. Sync only ever changes the schedule. If CloudKit is down, if you're on a plane, if iCloud is full — every device still rings on the last schedule it knew. Sync degrades to “your devices disagree”, never to “nothing rang”.

The hardest problem, deleted

Four devices holding one alarm need arbitration — which one rings, and what silences the other three when you dismiss. Aubade is iPhone-only, so none of that exists: one writer, one ringer, no merge. The design is kept on file rather than thrown away, because a Watch app brings every bit of it back.

The bar

How you tell hand-made from generated

Generated apps share one tell: every element got the same amount of attention. Uniform padding, uniform radius, one shadow on everything, emoji instead of icons, a settings screen that's an undifferentiated stack of toggles. It reads as generated because there's no evidence of anyone having decided what matters. Apps that feel hand-made show their priorities.

Hierarchy is visible. On any screen you can name the one thing it's for within half a second. Everything else is quieter.
The empty state is designed. So is the error state, the first-run state, the 200-tasks state, and the 3am state.
Motion is causal. A completed task doesn't fade — it slides into the done well, so you can see where it went.
Text is written. “No tasks today” is a placeholder. “Nothing left. You've got 3 hours back.” is a decision.
It's fast at the boring moments. Under 400ms to interactive, no dropped frames at 120Hz, a keyboard that arrives already focused. Speed is the most under-rated aesthetic property there is.
It respects the platform. Real menus and a menu-bar capture panel on Mac, three columns and pointer hover on iPad, Dynamic Type to AX5 everywhere. One SwiftUI blob identical on all three devices is its own kind of generated.
Details nobody asked for. Haptics tuned per gesture. Monospaced digits so the clock never jitters. A share sheet that produces something worth looking at.
Shape of the business

Paid once, no subscription, no accounts, no telemetry. Roughly $12 and $8, $16 together. There's no server cost to amortise, so a subscription would be rent-seeking and people can smell it — and a one-time price is the strongest signal available that the app is finished and won't grow a Pro tier inside itself. Privacy label target: Data Not Collected, literally.
